Devesh Dhole

I study turbulence in the solar wind: how energy cascading down from large scales collapses into thin current sheets near the scales of ions, and what a spacecraft sees when it flies through one.

PhD student in space plasma physics at Northumbria University, Newcastle upon Tyne, working with Luca Franci, Robert Wicks and Emanuele Papini.

How this works

It solves the equations of two-dimensional incompressible magnetohydrodynamics on a 128 × 128 periodic grid with a pseudo-spectral method:

∂tψ + u·∇ψ = η∇²ψ
∂tω + u·∇ω = B·∇J + ν∇²ω

Here ψ is the magnetic flux function, so field lines are its contours; ω is the vorticity and J = −∇²ψ the current. Colours and traces are scaled by their root-mean-square values. Magnetohydrodynamics treats the plasma as a single fluid, so it misses the ion-scale physics that hybrid particle-in-cell simulations capture, but its current sheets form for the same basic reason: turbulent flows push magnetic field lines together.

Research

The solar wind is a turbulent, almost collisionless plasma that spacecraft can measure from the inside, which makes it a natural laboratory for turbulence in space. My PhD combines kinetic simulations with those measurements to understand where turbulent energy goes at the smallest scales.

Finding current sheets

Turbulence in a collisionless plasma doesn't dissipate evenly. It concentrates into thin, intense current sheets, where magnetic energy is turned into heat and fast particles. I run hybrid particle-in-cell simulations with the CAMELIA code on the COSMA supercomputer in Durham, and compare methods for detecting these sheets automatically and measuring their thickness. One question I'm working on is how their properties change with plasma beta, the ratio of thermal to magnetic pressure.

Virtual spacecraft

A spacecraft records a single line through the plasma as it streams past. To compare simulations with Parker Solar Probe, Solar Orbiter and MMS on equal terms, I fly virtual spacecraft through simulation boxes and analyse the resulting time series with the same tools used on real data. Supernovae and gamma-ray bursts

Alongside my PhD, I collaborate on transient astronomy: photometry and light-curve modelling of supernovae that accompany gamma-ray bursts, image-subtraction pipelines, and observing proposals for the Liverpool Telescope.
